An Environmental Issue Investigation

Electronic Waste (E-Waste)

What happens to your old phones, laptops and game consoles when you're done with them?

62 million tonnes of e-waste were created worldwide in 2022 — that's like 1.55 million garbage trucks lined up around the Earth!

Start Exploring

What Is E-Waste?

E-waste is any thrown-away device that has a plug or a battery — phones, laptops, TVs, batteries, chargers, game consoles, toys with circuits, and lots more.

  • Phones
  • Laptops
  • TVs
  • Batteries
  • Printers
  • Headphones
  • Chargers
  • Smart Watches

Good stuff inside

Devices are full of valuable materials we could reuse instead of digging up more from the ground:

  • Gold
  • Copper
  • Silver
  • Aluminium
  • Rare earth metals

Bad stuff inside

They also contain toxic materials that can harm people and nature if they are not handled safely:

  • Lead
  • Mercury
  • Cadmium
  • Arsenic
  • Flame retardants

What Causes E-Waste?

E-waste is piling up for a few big reasons. Here are the main ones:

  • Upgrading too often

    Lots of people swap phones and gadgets for the newest model every year or two, even when the old one still works.

  • Made to break

    Some products are hard or expensive to repair. This is called "planned obsolescence" — designed to be replaced, not fixed.

  • Cheap electronics

    Very cheap gadgets and toys often wear out quickly, so they end up in the bin much sooner.

  • More people, more tech

    The world has more people using more devices than ever before, so we make more electronic waste too.

  • Hard to recycle

    Many people do not know where to take old devices, so they get thrown in the normal rubbish bin.

Where Is It Happening?

E-waste is a global problem — every country makes it. Here is how much each region made in 2022 (in millions of tonnes, Mt).

  • Asia24.9 Mt
  • Americas13.1 Mt
  • Europe12 Mt
  • Africa2.9 Mt
  • Oceania0.7 Mt

Did you know? In Australia, e-waste is growing about 3 times faster than regular household rubbish.

A lot of e-waste from wealthy countries — including Australia — is shipped overseas to places like Ghana, Nigeria and parts of Asia, where it is often taken apart by hand in unsafe ways.

Impact on the Environment

When e-waste is not handled properly, it hurts the planet in three big ways.

  • Soil & Water

    When e-waste sits in landfill, toxic metals like lead, mercury and cadmium can leak out into the soil and into the water underground.

  • Air

    Burning e-waste to get the metals out — common in unsafe recycling — releases poisonous fumes and smoke straight into the air.

  • Wasted Resources

    When we do not recycle, we have to mine brand-new gold, copper and rare metals from the Earth, which damages more land and nature.

Impact on Plants & Animals

Wildlife cannot choose where to live, so pollution from e-waste hits them hard.

  • Rivers & Fish

    Toxic chemicals can wash into rivers and lakes, poisoning fish and the animals that eat them.

  • The Food Chain

    Poisons build up as they move from small creatures to bigger animals — and can even end up in the food people eat.

  • Habitats

    Land and soil near e-waste dump sites become contaminated, making it hard for plants and animals to live there safely.

Impact on People

E-waste does not just harm nature — it affects people's health, homes and jobs too.

  • Health of Workers

    In some countries, people — sometimes even children — take apart e-waste by hand without safety gear. Breathing toxic smoke and touching chemicals can cause lung and skin problems.

  • Communities

    Families who live near e-waste dump sites can end up with polluted water and soil, which affects their health and daily lives.

  • Wasted Money & Jobs

    Billions of dollars of useful materials are thrown away instead of being recycled — money that could create safe "green jobs" instead.

E-waste keeps climbing every year. These are the totals made worldwide (in millions of tonnes).

201034 Mt

Global e-waste was already a growing problem.

A record high — the most e-waste ever made in one year.

203082 Mt*

Projected total if things keep going the way they are.

* 2030 figure is a projection.

More people know about e-waste and recycling schemes have grown — but e-waste is being made about 5 times faster than it is recycled. So the problem is still getting worse, not better.

What Is Being Done?

Lots of different groups are already working together to tackle the e-waste problem.

  • Governments

    Australia runs the National Television and Computer Recycling Scheme (started 2011). Companies that import TVs and computers must help pay for recycling — with a target rising to 80% by 2026–27.

  • Businesses

    Programs like MobileMuster let people hand back old mobile phones for free so they can be recycled safely instead of ending up in the bin.

  • Communities

    Local councils set up e-waste drop-off points and run collection events so families have an easy, safe place to take old devices.

  • Scientists

    Researchers and international organisations study e-waste and invent better, cleaner ways to recover valuable materials from old electronics.

What Solutions Could Help?

Here are three big ideas that could really cut down how much e-waste we make.

  1. "Right to Repair" Laws

    These laws make it easier and cheaper to fix devices — with spare parts and repair guides available. If we can repair things, we replace them less often, so we make far less e-waste.

  2. Better Recycling

    Building more recycling drop-off points and setting higher collection targets means more old devices get recycled properly instead of ending up in landfill.

  3. "Circular Economy" Design

    Companies can design electronics to be easily taken apart and recycled from the very start. Materials get reused again and again, instead of being thrown away.

What Can You Do?

Small actions add up. Here is a practical e-waste action checklist.

  • Take old phones and batteries to a proper e-waste drop-off point — not the bin!
  • Ask your parents about MobileMuster or your council e-waste collection days.
  • Look after your devices so they last much longer.
  • Donate or pass on working electronics instead of throwing them away.
  • Ask for repairs instead of replacements when something breaks.

Australian Case Study

Case File

Australia's National Television and Computer Recycling Scheme (NTCRS)

Australia · Started 2011

The NTCRS began in 2011 under the Product Stewardship Act. It requires TV and computer companies to help fund the recycling of their products. Collection targets started at 30% in 2012–13 and are rising toward 80% by 2026–27.

This is an example of "product stewardship" (also called extended producer responsibility) — the idea that companies share responsibility for what happens to their products at the end of their life, not just the people who buy them.

Why it matters: it shows that clear rules and shared responsibility can tackle e-waste on a national scale — not just by asking individuals to try harder, but by changing how the whole system works.

What Might Happen in the Future?

The future is not decided yet. It depends on the choices we make now.

If nothing changes

  • E-waste could reach 82 million tonnes by 2030.
  • Recycling rates could stay low.
  • More toxic pollution in soil, water and air.
  • More valuable resources wasted forever.

If we take action

  • Raising recycling to 60% by 2030 could bring benefits worth over $38 billion.
  • Fewer toxic chemicals released into nature.
  • More materials reused instead of mined.
  • New safe, green jobs created.
